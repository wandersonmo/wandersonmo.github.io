Cut the slop

It's been getting harder to keep up with AI responses. Ask a simple question and you get a full article: a pile of caveats, a recap of what you just asked, and an offer to help further.

Most of the time, we don't need all of it. We just want to get to the other side: the answer, the next step, whatever unblocks us.

People solved this problem long before AI. Wherever attention was scarce and the stakes were high, they built conventions for communicating without noise. The military puts the bottom line up front (BLUF). Aircraft maintenance manuals are written in ASD-STE100, a controlled English dictionary. Consultants structure arguments with the pyramid principle.

AI models were trained on text full of these conventions, so established terms work as compressed pointers to them. Ask for a "TL;DR" and you'll usually get a short bulleted summary, without ever explaining what TL;DR means.

That's how I've been working for a while: a short list of easy-to-remember keywords at the end of a prompt that reshape the answer. My day job is troubleshooting Spark workloads. I'm often in the middle of a messy root-cause analysis under a tight mitigation deadline, and I need to grasp a complex issue at a glance. Keywords are how I get there.

This post covers the ones I use. I've tested each one on a handful of problems, alone and then stacked together, so you can see exactly what each one does.

Four layers

Each one controls a different part of the answer, and they fall into four layers: structure, register, visual encoding, and epistemics.

Bottom line up front. The answer goes in the first line, and details follow.

p50 is the median: half your requests are faster. p90 is the line the slowest 10% cross.

Together they show the typical case and the tail that averages hide.

Inflation happens when spending outruns what the economy can produce, or when supply shocks push costs up.

Expectations decide whether the jump fades or sticks.

Hegel's dialectic is how a concept develops by running into its own limits.

Each one-sided idea, pushed far enough, turns into its opposite. What comes out keeps both.

The transaction log is an ordered list of JSON commits that defines what the table is.

Readers replay it to get a snapshot. Writers append to it, one version at a time.

One governing answer, with the supporting points grouped under it.

Percentiles describe the spread, not just the middle.

  • p50: half the values are at or below it (the median).
  • p90: 90% are at or below it, so 1 in 10 is slower.
  • The gap between them shows how bad the tail is.

Inflation comes from too much demand, too little supply, or expectations that feed on themselves.

  • Demand-pull: spending outruns output.
  • Cost-push: shocks raise costs, like energy in 2022.
  • Expectations: wages and prices build in more inflation.

The dialectic is a concept's own movement, not a method applied from outside.

  • Understanding fixes a concept as stable and one-sided.
  • Thought through, the concept passes into its opposite.
  • The result keeps what was true in both.

The log turns plain files into a table with transactions.

  • Each commit is one JSON file, named by its version.
  • Only one writer can create each version.
  • Checkpoints keep replays short.
